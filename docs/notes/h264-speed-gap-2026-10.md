# H.264: where the remaining gap to FFmpeg comes from (2026-10-27)

A measurement, no code change. The question: the H.264 decoder takes 2 to 3 times FFmpeg's single-threaded time on
every stream class, after rounds of fixing one hot spot at a time flattened the profile (README, Open). Is there one
mechanism behind it, and what should change next?

**Answer.** The gap is the number of instructions executed: 3.6 to 4.2 times FFmpeg's, on all three streams measured,
while each of our instructions takes a little less time than FFmpeg's (on the 720p streams we run at 1.2 to 1.3
times its instruction rate). Not memory: simulated cache misses per frame are the same as FFmpeg's or fewer. Not
bounds checks, write barriers, generic instantiation, interface dispatch or call overhead as such (each measured below). The extra
instructions come from how the work is cut up: per sample, per row, per 4x4 block, per edge and per partition, each
call working out again what is constant for the macroblock (its position, by division; its neighbours, by coordinate
lookups; its QPs; the block width, by branches in every row store). FFmpeg does that work once per macroblock (one
neighbour cache, filled once) and calls fixed-size kernels. So no single function dominates: every stage executes
more than FFmpeg's, the most where its work is cut into the smallest steps. The proposed first PR (end of this
note) applies the per-macroblock approach to inter prediction's per-partition path, the stage with the most excess
instructions and no open change in its files.

## Method

Machine: the 4-core cloud VM (Intel Xeon at 2.8 GHz, no SMT, 1 MiB L2 per core, 33 MiB L3; no hardware performance
counters, so `perf stat -e cache-misses` is not available). .NET 10.0.113 (runtime 10.0.13 under valgrind), FFmpeg
6.1.1 (Ubuntu's, with its `libavcodec60-dbgsym` symbols from ddebs.ubuntu.com so its profile resolves to functions).
Streams:

- `h264-bench-720p.mp4`: Constrained Baseline, CAVLC, one I and 49 P pictures, 1280x720.
- A 720p libx264 default stream (High, CABAC, B pictures, 8x8 transform), made from the same source as the bench sample:
  `ffmpeg -f lavfi -i "testsrc2=size=1280x720:rate=25,scroll=h=0.005,noise=alls=6:allf=t" -t 2 -c:v libx264
  -pix_fmt yuv420p` (not committed; README's 720p classes said the same of the others).
- `h264-high-default.mp4`: libx264's High defaults at 320x240.

Tools, each for what it can and cannot see (CLAUDE.md, Timing):

1. **Wall time**: `tools/compare.sh` (two runs each; 20, 10 and 200 loops).
2. **Where the time goes**: perf (`-e cpu-clock -F 4000`, `DOTNET_PerfMapEnabled=1 DOTNET_EnableWriteXorExecute=0
   DOTNET_TieredCompilation=0`), flat by function; and with `-g` (frame pointers work for JIT code) and
   `DOTNET_ReadyToRun=0` (so that `SpanHelpers.Memmove` and the other CoreLib helpers have names), each sample assigned
   to a stage by the first frame from the leaf that names one (`DeblockRows` and below: deblocking; `ReadResidual`
   and below: residual parsing; and so on). FFmpeg is assigned by its leaf function; its asm's local labels
   (`..@3485.branch_instr`) are resolved to their functions through the debug symbols.
3. **How many instructions**: callgrind on a **Native AOT build of mcdec** (`dotnet publish tools/ManagedCodecs.Cli -c
   Release -r linux-x64 -p:PublishAot=true -p:IlcInstructionSet=x86-64-v3`). This sidesteps CLAUDE.md's warning that
   the JIT falls back to MinOpts under valgrind: AOT code is compiled before valgrind sees it, and its speed is the
   JIT build's (7.79 against 7.6 to 8.0 ms a frame on the 720p bench sample). Loops N minus loops 1 gives the steady
   state. The AOT compiler inlines a little differently from the JIT (`AddClipped` and `Neighbor` are calls there), so
   function-level counts differ; stage totals do not depend on it. `--dump-instr=yes` with `objdump -d` of each binary
   classifies the executed instructions (vector, divisions, calls, branches).
4. **Memory**: cachegrind with a 48 KiB L1 and a 1 MiB last level (the per-core L2). For ours this runs the JIT's
   MinOpts code, which adds stack traffic (L1 hits) but reads and writes the pictures and tables as optimized code does;
   the miss counts are what matters.

FFmpeg's "header syntax" includes its motion vector prediction, which `ff_h264_decode_mb_cavlc` and `_cabac` inline,
so read that row and the next together. Its "runtime, kernel, other" is mostly the command line's threads and, on the
short stream, reopening the file for every loop.

Read across the three: the edge filters and residual parsing, each one tight kernel, run at 2.1 to 3.1 times FFmpeg's
instructions, and header syntax with MV prediction (taken together, as FFmpeg inlines one into the other) at 3.1 to
3.3. The stages made of many small steps per sample, block or edge run higher: inter prediction 4.3 to 6.4 times, the
bS derivation 5.6 to 9.5, the transform and add 11 to 16. No stage is cheaper than FFmpeg's, and none holds more than
about a quarter of the excess instructions.

## What was ruled out, and how

- **Memory traffic.** Cachegrind, steady state on `h264-bench-720p.mp4`, per frame: L1 data misses 130 k (ours)
  against 149 k (FFmpeg), last-level misses 75 k against 81 k. The misses are the same; we do not touch more memory.
  They do cost us: in the motion compensation kernels, most samples land on the first load of each reference row (by
  instruction, from perf samples matched with the JIT's listing: `CopyInside` 80%, `HalfHorizontal` and `HalfVertical`
  50%, `Bilinear` 43%), about 10% of the decoder's time waiting on reference reads. FFmpeg hides some of it with
  software prefetch (its `ff_prefetch` is 1.8% of its profile): it prefetches the reference area four macroblocks
  ahead. A scratch copy doing the same for luma and chroma (`Sse.Prefetch0`, four luma and two chroma rows a
  macroblock) took the kernels' samples down by about 3,400 of 125,000 (2.7%) but cost 1,900 itself (1.5%; not
  traced further), net -1.3%; wall clock -1.6% median of 6 interleaved pairs, spread ±5%. Worth
  folding into the per-partition rewrite below with its address arithmetic made cheap, not alone.
- **Bounds checks.** Counted in the JIT's listings of the hot methods (jumps to a `RNGCHKFAIL` block): none in the
  motion compensation filters and copies or the deblocking filter's vector code; 15 in `InverseTransformAdd`, 18 in
  `ReadResidualBlock`, 26 in `PredictPartition` (its jagged `_refPic[list][part]`, `_mvX[list][block]` reads). They
  are part of the per-call work below, not a cost of their own: the methods carrying most of them are small shares.
- **Call overhead as such.** Samples on function entries, push/pop/ret and the instruction after a call: 10.3% of ours
  against 8.3% of FFmpeg's, measured the same way (FFmpeg's from `objdump` of libavcodec). The share is about the same;
  what differs is how much work there is in all.
- **Write barriers**: 0.23 M instructions a frame, from storing a `Picture` reference per partition (`StoreMotion`;
  0.6% of the time). **Interface dispatch**: none per macroblock. **Generic instantiation**: `PictureDecoder<byte>` is a
  value-type instantiation; no hot method has a runtime generic lookup (`CORINFO_HELP_RUNTIMEHANDLE_*`), and the
  `typeof(TPixel)` branches fold. **GC**: allocation clearing is 0.24 M instructions a frame.
- **Divisions**: 65 k integer divisions a frame (18 a macroblock), all `mbAddr / _widthInMbs` or `% _widthInMbs`:
  `DeblockRows` 30 k, `LumaPlace` 19 k, `PartitionPlace` 8 k, `PredictPartition` 4 k, `MacroblockDone` 4 k. At 12 to 20
  cycles each that is at most 3.5 to 6% of a 720p frame if none overlapped; the functions holding them are 8% of the
  profile, most of it other work. A
  real but small item, best removed with the code around it.

What the executed instructions are (callgrind with `--dump-instr`, the 73% of ours and 86% of FFmpeg's whose
addresses `objdump` resolved): vector instructions are 16% of ours and 35% of FFmpeg's, compares and branches 23%
against 11%, calls, returns, pushes and pops 13% against 4%. In absolute numbers we execute more vector instructions
than FFmpeg (about 9 M a frame against 5 M), and about five times its scalar ones. The compares and branches are per
sample and per row: `AddClipped` (one call a residual sample, 155 k a frame, clamping by branches), `StorePartial`
(one a predicted row, 115 k a frame, a switch on the width each time), `FetchWindow`'s clamped path (282 calls, 4,200
instructions each), `FilterEdge` (36 k calls), `ChromaQp` (40 k calls, 11 a macroblock, the same few QPs again).

## Candidates, ranked

Shares are perf's self time on the JIT build (720p CAVLC / 720p CABAC-B / 320x240 High). A change cannot gain more
than the share of the functions it changes (CLAUDE.md, Timing), so that share is the ceiling; the estimate assumes
the changed code ends at about twice FFmpeg's instructions for the same work, as our tight stages already are.

| # | Change | Functions (share) | Instructions now | Ceiling | Estimate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | **Inter prediction's per-partition path**: place each macroblock once (no divisions), dispatch each partition straight to a kernel for its width and fractional position, one reference wait a macroblock, row stores of a fixed width, the prefetch above | `PredictPartition`, `PredictComponent`, `PredictLuma`, `PredictChroma` (setup), `PartitionPlace`, `LumaPlace`, `ChromaPlace`, `Weighting`, `Store`, `StorePartial` (7.7 / 6.8 / 6.0%), plus the reference stalls the prefetch hides (2.7% measured) | about 1,100 a partition before the filters (`PredictPartition` 409, `PredictComponent` 3 x 66, `PredictLuma` 137, `PredictChroma` 2 x 110, placement 140), against about 200 in FFmpeg's `mc_part_420`; 3,900 partitions a 720p frame | 8 to 10% | 4 to 6% |
| 2 | **A per-macroblock neighbour context**: fill the left and upper neighbours' availability, nC, motion vectors, references, intra modes and CABAC flags once a macroblock into a fixed layout, and read it at fixed offsets in nC, MV prediction, ctxIdxInc and bS | `Neighbor` (inlined in `ReadBlock`), `MotionNeighbor`, `PredictMv`, `LumaNc`, `CodedBlockFlagInc`, `CabacCbp`'s and `CabacMvd`'s neighbour reads, `StoreMotion`, the direct modes' neighbour reads (5.9 / 14.4 / 12.8%, plus part of `ReadBlock`) | `Neighbor` 32 k calls a frame (9 a macroblock), `MotionNeighbor` 14 k; the MV and bookkeeping stage at 6.4 to 7.8 times FFmpeg's instructions | 6 to 14% | 3 to 7% |

| 4 | **bS and QP once a macroblock**: the edge QPs and chroma QPs from a per-macroblock table, bS for all 16 edge segments from the neighbour context (2) | `MotionStrength`, `Far`, `HasUniformMotion`, `MacroblockEdgeStrengths`, `InternalStrengths`, `BoundaryStrength`, `ChromaQp`, `ChromaParameters` (5.8 / 5.3 / 5.8%) | 5.6 to 9.5 times; `ChromaQp` 11 calls a macroblock | 5 to 6% | 2 to 3% |
| 5 | Output frames without the copy (`Crop`): hand out the decoded picture, pooled and reference counted | `Crop`, `Memmove` (3.5 / 2.5 / 2.6%) | one copy of every frame | 3.5% | 3% |

Notes on them:

- 4 edits `PictureDecoder.Deblock.cs`, which has an open change by another session (as of this note), and so do the
  divisions in `DeblockRows`: wait for it.
- 2 is the largest ceiling on CABAC streams and the most work: it touches CAVLC and CABAC parsing, MV prediction, the
  direct modes and (with 4) the deblocking filter, and MBAFF frames need the pair-aware variant of the fill (Table
  6-4). Past attempts at caching neighbours in MBAFF (docs/conformance.md, Performance notes) added a cache on top of
  the lookups and lost; this replaces the lookups, filled once a macroblock. It wants a design note before code.
- 5 is an API decision (who owns an output frame, and for how long), not only speed: for the owner.
- 6 was looked at before and left (each macroblock would clear its own state instead, about as many bytes).
- Together 1 to 4 reach 20 to 40% of the frame by their ceilings, which is the size of change the gap needs: the
  gap is spread over every stage, and only a change of approach in several of them closes a large part of it.

## Recommended first PR

**1, inter prediction's per-partition path**, for the owner to decide:

- It is the stage with the most excess instructions (+11 M a 720p frame, of +43 M in all) and the largest single
  share (24 to 30% of the time on the 720p streams), and its per-partition glue is about five times FFmpeg's.
- It is contained: `PictureDecoder.Inter.cs` and `InterPrediction*.cs` (neither has an open change; `VectorZip.cs` and
  `PictureDecoder.Deblock.cs` are untouched), and the filters themselves stay as they are, compared against the
  by-the-definition `PredictLumaReference` as today, with every conformance sample's MD5.
- It is measurable below the wall clock's noise: instructions a partition on the AOT build under callgrind (the
  target: under 500 from about 1,100), the changed functions' perf share before and after back to back, and
  `compare.yml` on hosted runners for the wall clock. Estimated 4 to 6% of a 720p frame, ceiling 8 to 10%.
- It is the pattern the rest needs (work out per macroblock what is constant for it, call fixed-size kernels), so 3
  and 2 follow it.

Then 2 as a design note first, 3, and 4 once the deblocking change has merged.

## A tool worth keeping

Callgrind on a Native AOT build counts the instructions of optimized code, which CLAUDE.md says valgrind cannot do with
the JIT (MinOpts). It repeats to the instruction, sees through inlining differences only at the function level, and
takes about a minute for 150 frames of 720p. CLAUDE.md's Timing section could name it next to the JIT one.
